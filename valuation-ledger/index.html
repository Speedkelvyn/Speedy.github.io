How to Use This Book

In plain English

Imagine you inherited a small bakery and someone offers to buy it from you. How would you decide what price is fair? You would probably think: "How much cash does this bakery put in my pocket each year? How long will that last? How sure am I?" — Congratulations, you have just invented valuation. Everything in this book is that bakery question, asked with more and more precision, about bigger and bigger companies.

The book is organized as a single argument in seven parts. Part I teaches you the raw material: what money over time is worth, and how to read a company's three financial statements like an X-ray. Part II asks how risky a stock is and what return investors demand for bearing that risk. Part III does the same for bonds — what lenders charge. Part IV asks how the mix of debt and equity affects value, and blends both costs into one number: the WACC. Part V is the engine room: forecast the company's future cash, discount it, and arrive at a share price. Part VI is honesty insurance: how wrong might we be, and what does the market believe? Part VII rebuilds everything in Python so the computer does the arithmetic and you do the thinking.

Part I · Chapter 01

The One Big Idea of Valuation

In plain English

A company is a machine that turns effort into cash. Its value today is simply all the cash it will ever hand its owners, translated into today's dollars. Two things complicate the translation: cash that arrives later is worth less (you'd rather have $100 now than in ten years), and cash that's uncertain is worth less (a promised $100 from a shaky business isn't really $100). Valuation is the craft of making both adjustments carefully.

Formally, the value of any asset is the present value of its expected future cash flows:

$$V_0=\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\frac{E[CF_t]}{(1+r)^t}$$

Every chapter of this book exists to answer one of the three questions hiding inside that formula:

  1. What are the cash flows? (statements, forecasting, free cash flow — Parts I and V)
  2. What discount rate r matches their risk? (beta, CAPM, bonds, WACC — Parts II, III, IV)
  3. How wrong might we be? (scenarios, simulation, cross-checks — Part VI)

Price is not value

Price is what the stock market quotes this second; value is what the business is worth based on its fundamentals. The whole premise of fundamental analysis is that the two can drift apart — fear, hype, neglect — and tend to converge over time. A valuation model is a machine for estimating value independently of price, so that the gap becomes a signal: value far above price suggests a buy (with a safety margin); far below, an avoid. If your "independent" estimate secretly borrows the market price — say, by choosing growth assumptions that happen to justify it — the model only flatters the market. Chapter 35 turns that borrowing into a deliberate tool called the reverse DCF.

Two doors into the same house

A company has two kinds of money-claimants: lenders (debt) and owners (equity). You can value the whole house and subtract the mortgage, or value the owner's slice directly:

  • Enterprise approach: discount the cash available to everyone (called FCFF) at the blended rate everyone demands (WACC), then subtract what the lenders are owed. This book leads with this door because it cleanly separates "how good is the business" from "how is it financed."
  • Equity approach: discount the cash left for shareholders only (FCFE) at the shareholders' required return. Done consistently, both doors open into the same room — and we will use the second as a built-in error check.

The three families of valuation

Intrinsic (DCF) builds value from your own forecast — richest in information, heaviest in assumptions. Relative (multiples) values by comparison: "similar companies sell for 12× their earnings, so…" — fast and market-anchored, but it imports the market's mistakes. Contingent-claims (options) values flexibility — the right to expand, abandon, or wait. Professionals triangulate all three; the "football field" chart in Chapter 38 is the picture of that triangulation.

Check yourself
Because the expected cash is lower than the promise and investors demand compensation for bearing the chance of disappointment. In the formula, that compensation lives in a higher discount rate r, which shrinks present value.
Price is the market's current quote; value is what the cash-generating power of the business justifies. Valuation estimates value independently so the gap between the two becomes an investment signal.
Part I · Chapter 02

Time Value of Money: The Discounting Machinery

In plain English

If I owe you $100, would you rather have it today or in three years? Today, obviously — you could put it in the bank and have more than $100 in three years. So $100 arriving in three years must be worth less than $100 today. "Discounting" is just calculating exactly how much less, using the interest rate as the exchange rate between today-money and future-money. Every valuation in this book is, at bottom, a long exercise in this single conversion.

The machinery

Money grows forward by compounding and shrinks backward by discounting:

$$FV=P(1+r)^t \qquad\qquad PV=\frac{CF_t}{(1+r)^t}$$

If interest compounds m times a year, \(FV=P(1+\tfrac{r}{m})^{mt}\). Let m grow without limit and you reach continuous compounding, the convention used in the Python/Hilpisch world and in option pricing: \(FV=Pe^{rt}\), \(PV=CFe^{-rt}\).

Two shortcuts cover most repeated cash flows. An annuity (the same payment C for T years) is worth

$$PV=C\cdot\frac{1-(1+r)^{-T}}{r}$$

and a growing perpetuity (a payment growing at rate g forever, with g < r) is worth

$$PV=\frac{C_1}{r-g}$$

That last little formula will reappear in Chapter 33 wearing a suit, under the name terminal value — where it will carry 60–80% of an entire company's worth. Respect it now.

Worked example

$100 arriving in 3 years, at r = 8%: \(PV = 100/1.08^3 = 100/1.2597 = \$79.38\). Under continuous compounding at the same stated 8%: \(100e^{-0.24} = \$78.66\) — slightly lower, because continuous compounding compounds harder.

A perpetuity paying $5 next year and growing 2% forever, at r = 8%: \(PV = 5/(0.08-0.02) = \$83.33\). Nudge growth to 3% and PV jumps to \(5/0.05 = \$100\) — a one-point change in g moved value by 20%. Keep that sensitivity in mind: it is the reason terminal-value assumptions get a whole disciplinary chapter.

Where people go wrong
  • Rate/period mismatch: monthly cash flows need a monthly rate — \((1+r)^{1/12}-1\), not r ÷ 12 (unless quoted as an APR).
  • Nominal vs real: discount nominal (inflation-included) cash at nominal rates and real cash at real rates. The Fisher relation connects them: \((1+r_{nom})=(1+r_{real})(1+\pi)\).
  • Timing: standard discounting pretends all cash lands on December 31. The mid-year convention (use exponent t − 0.5) reflects cash arriving through the year and lifts PV by roughly 4% at r = 8%.

If cash grew as fast as (or faster than) the discount rate forever, each year's present value would never shrink and the sum would be infinite. Economically: nothing outgrows the economy forever; mathematically: the geometric series only converges when g < r.

Part I · Chapter 03

Reading Financial Statements: The Raw Material

In plain English

Companies publish three report cards. The income statement answers "how did the year go?" — like a video of performance. The balance sheet answers "what do we own and owe right now?" — a photograph taken at midnight on the last day. The cash flow statement answers "where did the actual cash go?" — necessary because accountants count sales when invoiced, not when paid, so 'profit' and 'cash in the bank' can tell very different stories. Valuation needs all three: earning power from the video, capital tied up from the photo, and truth-serum from the cash statement.

The income statement, restated for analysis

Reported statements come in many shapes; analysts restate them into one standard ladder:

Revenue
− Cost of goods sold              → Gross profit
− SG&A  − R&D  − Depreciation & amortization
= EBIT   ← the operating-profit line everything pivots on
− Interest expense                → Pre-tax income
− Taxes                           → Net income

EBIT is the boundary line. Everything above it is the business; everything below is the financing of the business. Enterprise valuation lives above the line. We also normalize: strip one-time charges (restructurings, lawsuits, impairments) so EBIT reflects sustainable earning power, and decide a consistent policy for amortization of acquired intangibles (most analysts add it back — it is an echo of past acquisitions, not a cost of running this year's business).

The balance sheet, sorted by job instead of by liquidity

Accountants sort assets by how fast they turn into cash. Analysts re-sort by function: which items do the operating work, and which are financing? The operating side, netted, is called invested capital (IC) — the money actually tied up in running the business:

$$IC=\underbrace{(\text{Receivables}+\text{Inventory}-\text{Payables \& accruals})}_{\text{operating working capital}}+\text{Net PP\&E}\;(+\,\text{goodwill, by policy})$$

Two judgment calls matter. Goodwill: include it and ROIC measures management's skill including the prices paid for acquisitions; exclude it and ROIC measures the underlying business engine. Compute both — they answer different questions. Cash: a working float (≈ 2% of revenue) is operating; the rest is excess cash, a non-operating asset that re-enters the story in Chapter 34's bridge.

The cash flow statement and the articulation check

The three statements are one machine: net income flows into equity, capital spending builds PP&E, working-capital swings tie revenue to receivables. The most valuable error-detector in all of financial modeling is to rebuild the change in cash from the other two statements and confirm it matches:

$$\Delta\text{Cash}=NI+D\&A-\Delta NWC-CapEx+\text{net borrowing}+\text{equity issued}-\text{dividends}$$

If your derived ΔCash disagrees with the balance sheet's cash line, the model is leaking somewhere. We will hard-wire this articulation check into every forecast year of the model.

Meet Meridian — Year 0 ($ millions)

Revenue 10,000 · COGS 3,100 · SG&A 3,500 · R&D 1,000 · D&A 400 → EBIT 2,000 · interest 300 · tax 22% → net income 1,326.

Balance sheet: cash 800 · receivables 1,800 · inventory 1,500 · net PP&E 2,600 · goodwill & intangibles 12,000 · payables & accruals 1,900 · debt 9,000 · equity 7,800.

Derived: working capital = 1,800 + 1,500 − 1,900 = 1,400. Operating cash = 2% × 10,000 = 200, so excess cash = 600. NOPAT = 2,000 × (1 − 0.22) = 1,560. Invested capital = 16,200 with goodwill, 4,200 without. So ROIC = 9.6% with goodwill vs 37.1% without — the classic signature of a serial acquirer: a superb underlying engine whose excess returns were largely paid away, upfront, to the sellers of the companies it bought.

Where people go wrong
  • Using net income where EBIT/NOPAT belongs — it smuggles financing into an operating measure.
  • Treating all cash as excess (overstates non-operating assets) or all as operating (understates them).
  • "Plugging" the balance sheet to force it to balance — the plug always buries a real error that the articulation check would have caught.
Check yourself
Accrual profit books sales when earned, not when collected. If receivables and inventory balloon (cash going out to fund growth) while customers pay slowly, the income statement smiles while the bank account empties. The cash flow statement exists precisely to expose this gap.
For forecasting organic growth, the no-goodwill figure approximates the return on new capital the business itself deploys. The with-goodwill figure judges management's full record, acquisitions included. Chapter 31 must take an explicit stand on the incremental ROIC.
Part I · Chapter 04

The Statements in Depth: Line by Line

In plain English

Chapter 3 gave you the map; this chapter walks every street. Each line of the three statements answers a specific question — "has the customer paid yet?", "how worn out are the machines?", "what does the company owe but hasn't been billed for?" — and once you know which question each line answers, a 10-K stops being a wall of numbers and becomes a conversation. We end by performing the analyst's party trick: rebuilding a company's entire cash flow statement using only its income statement and two balance sheets.

The income statement, line by line

  • Revenue — recorded when earned (goods delivered, service performed), not when cash arrives. That single accrual principle creates most of the gaps between profit and cash.
  • COGS — costs directly tied to what was sold: materials, factory labor, factory overhead. Revenue − COGS = gross profit, the purest read on pricing power.
  • SG&A — selling, general & administrative: salesforce, headquarters, marketing. R&D — expensed as incurred under US GAAP even though it builds the future (one reason ROIC can mislead for research-heavy firms).
  • Depreciation & amortization — this year's slice of past capital spending (machines) and past acquisitions (intangibles), spread over useful lives. Non-cash, but absolutely a real economic cost over time.
  • Operating income (EBIT) — the business line. Below it: interest expense/income (financing), unusual items, then pre-tax income, the tax provision (book taxes, which differ from cash taxes via deferred items), and net income.
  • EPS, basic and diluted — net income per share; diluted assumes in-the-money options and convertibles become shares (the count Chapter 34 will insist on).

The balance sheet, drawer by drawer

Assets (what the firm controls), in order of liquidity: cash & equivalents → marketable securities → accounts receivable (sold, not yet collected) → inventory (raw materials, work-in-process, finished goods) → prepaid expenses → then long-term: PP&E shown three ways (gross cost − accumulated depreciation = net) → intangibles & goodwill (goodwill = price paid in acquisitions above identifiable asset value; never amortized, tested annually for impairment).

Liabilities (what it owes): accounts payable (suppliers' unpaid bills) → accrued expenses (wages earned by staff, not yet paid) → deferred revenue (customers paid in advance — the firm owes them work, which is why cash received sits as a liability) → short-term debt & current portion of long-term debt → then long-term debt, lease liabilities, deferred taxes, pension obligations.

Equity (the residual): common stock & paid-in capital (cash ever raised from shareholders) → retained earnings (every dollar of net income ever earned, minus every dividend ever paid — the income statement's permanent memory) → treasury stock (negative: buybacks) → accumulated other comprehensive income (currency translation, pension remeasurements). The iron law: Assets = Liabilities + Equity, always, by construction.

The cash flow statement, three engines

  • Operating (CFO) — indirect method: start from net income, add back non-cash charges (D&A, stock compensation, impairments), then adjust for working-capital swings: a rise in receivables or inventory consumed cash; a rise in payables or deferred revenue released it. (The direct method — listing cash received and paid — is rarer but identical in total.)
  • Investing (CFI) — capital expenditures, acquisitions, purchases/sales of securities. The "building the future" section.
  • Financing (CFF) — borrowings and repayments, share issuance, buybacks, dividends. The "dealing with the financiers" section.

CFO + CFI + CFF = the change in the cash line on the balance sheet. That equality is Chapter 3's articulation check wearing its formal clothes.

Where to actually read all this

In a 10-K: Item 7 (MD&A) is management's own narrative of the numbers; Item 8 holds the statements and — crucially — the footnotes: revenue recognition policy, debt schedule with maturities and rates (Chapter 22's raw material), lease tables, pension assumptions, segment data, share-count reconciliation. Professionals read footnotes first; the statements are the summary, the footnotes are the truth.

The party trick — deriving MMD's cash flow statement

Given Year 0 → Year 1: NI 1,420 · D&A 432 · receivables +144 · inventory +120 · payables & accruals +152 · CapEx 540 · net borrowing 0 · dividends 426.

CFO = 1,420 + 432 − 144 − 120 + 152 = 1,740 · CFI = −540 · CFF = −426 → ΔCash = +774. If the balance sheet's cash line moved by anything else, a line item is missing or wrong — the model leak detector in action.

Where people go wrong
  • Reading rising deferred revenue as a problem — for subscription businesses it is prepaid demand, among the healthiest liabilities that exist.
  • Forgetting accumulated depreciation: comparing gross PP&E across firms with different asset ages.
  • Treating stock-based compensation as free because it is non-cash — it dilutes, and dilution is the shareholders' expense.
Check yourself
Month zero: cash +1,200 (CFO), deferred revenue +1,200 (liability) — no revenue yet. Month six: revenue 600 recognized on the income statement, deferred revenue down to 600. The income statement spreads what the cash statement booked instantly — accrual accounting in one transaction.
Into retained earnings on the balance sheet — the equity account that accumulates all earnings ever kept. That is the formal channel by which the income statement feeds the balance sheet each period.

Ratios & Value Drivers

In plain English

Raw statement numbers are like a person's weight without their height — meaningless until scaled. Ratios are the scaling: profit per dollar of capital, sales per dollar of assets, days a customer takes to pay. More importantly, ratios are the dials a forecast actually turns. You never forecast "receivables = 1,978"; you forecast "customers keep paying in about 66 days" and let the model do the rest.

The master identity

$$ROIC=\frac{NOPAT}{IC}=\underbrace{\frac{NOPAT}{Sales}}_{\text{margin}}\times\underbrace{\frac{Sales}{IC}}_{\text{capital turnover}}$$

Every business strategy lives in this split: pharma earns through fat margins on slow-turning capital; a grocery chain earns through razor margins on fast-turning capital. The equity-side cousin is DuPont: \(ROE = \text{margin}\times\text{asset turnover}\times\text{leverage}\) — and that third term is the warning label, because ROE can be manufactured with borrowed money even when the underlying ROIC is mediocre.

Working capital in days

$$DSO=\frac{\text{Receivables}}{\text{Revenue}}\times365\quad DIO=\frac{\text{Inventory}}{\text{COGS}}\times365\quad DPO=\frac{\text{Payables}}{\text{COGS}}\times365$$

The cash conversion cycle CCC = DSO + DIO − DPO is how many days a dollar stays trapped in operations between paying suppliers and collecting from customers. These "days" dials become the working-capital forecast in Chapter 31.

The most important equation in forecasting

$$g=\text{Reinvestment Rate}\times ROIC\qquad\text{where }RR=\frac{CapEx-D\&A+\Delta NWC}{NOPAT}$$

Growth is never free. A company that wants to grow at g while earning ROIC on new projects must plough back g/ROIC of its profit. This identity will discipline the forecast (Chapter 31) and rescue the terminal value from fantasy (Chapter 33). Finally, the credit dials — interest coverage = EBIT/interest, and Debt/EBITDA — feed the synthetic credit rating in Chapter 22.

Meridian's dials

Margin 15.6% × turnover 0.62 = ROIC 9.6% ✓. DSO 65.7 days · DIO 176.6 (hospitals hold consigned inventory — normal for medtech) · DPO 223.7 → CCC 18.6 days. Coverage 2,000/300 = 6.7× · Debt/EBITDA 3.75×. Sustainability: reinvesting 40% of NOPAT at 37% incremental ROIC supports g ≈ 14.8%; at 9.6% it supports only 3.8%. The forecast must choose — and defend — its incremental ROIC.

Where people go wrong
  • Comparing ratios across firms with different accounting (lease treatment!) without adjusting.
  • Year-end balance-sheet values against full-year flows — use average balances for turnover ratios.
  • Extrapolating a ratio's trend without naming the economic force behind it.
Check yourself
RR = g/ROIC = 12/24 = 50% of NOPAT. If its forecast shows growth of 12% with only 25% reinvestment, it is silently claiming a 48% incremental ROIC — a claim that should be made out loud or corrected.
DuPont's third factor is leverage. Borrowing amplifies ROE mechanically while adding risk. Check whether the quality comes from margin and turnover (operations) or from the leverage multiplier (financing).

NPV & the Investment Decision Rules

In plain English

Here is the deepest principle in finance — Berk & DeMarzo build their entire book on it: identical things must sell for identical prices (the Law of One Price), so the value of any decision is what its cash flows would cost to buy in the market. From that single idea falls the master rule: compute the net present value — the value of what you get minus what you pay, all in today's dollars — and take every project where it's positive. Every other rule you'll meet (IRR, payback) is a popular shortcut that sometimes agrees with NPV and sometimes walks you off a cliff. This chapter teaches the rule and maps the cliffs.

The NPV rule

$$NPV=\sum_{t=0}^{T}\frac{CF_t}{(1+r)^t}=-I_0+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{CF_t}{(1+r)^t}$$

Accept if NPV > 0; among mutually exclusive options, take the highest NPV. The discount rate r is the project's opportunity cost of capital — what investors could earn elsewhere at the same risk. NPV is measured in dollars of value created today, which is why it is the only rule that always ranks correctly: dollars add; percentages don't.

IRR — useful, seductive, treacherous

The internal rate of return is the r that makes NPV zero — the project's break-even cost of capital. Rule: accept if IRR > r. It agrees with NPV for normal, standalone projects. The three cliffs:

  1. Non-conventional cash flows. If signs flip more than once (invest, receive, then pay cleanup costs), there can be multiple IRRs or none — the rule simply breaks.
  2. Scale. A food cart can return 100% on $1,000 (NPV maybe $900); a factory returns 20% on $10M (NPV maybe $2M). IRR picks the cart; your bank account prefers the factory.
  3. Timing & reinvestment. IRR implicitly assumes interim cash reinvests at the IRR — heroic for high-IRR projects. Modified IRR (reinvest at r) patches this.

The supporting cast

Payback period — years to recover the investment. Simple, beloved by operators, and blind twice over: it ignores the time value of money and everything that happens after payback. Use only as a liquidity screen. Profitability index — PV of future cash per dollar invested, PI = PV/I₀ — the right tool when capital is rationed: rank by PI and fund down the list until the budget runs out.

When IRR and NPV disagree

r = 10%. Project A: invest 1,000, receive 1,500 in one year → IRR 50%, NPV = −1,000 + 1,500/1.1 = +364. Project B: invest 10,000, receive 13,000 in one year → IRR 30%, NPV = −10,000 + 13,000/1.1 = +1,818. IRR shouts A; NPV calmly points at B — and B makes you five times richer. Percentages flatter the small; dollars pay the bills.

Part I · Chapter 07

Capital Budgeting: From Project to Cash Flow

In plain English

NPV is easy once you have the cash flows; the craft is deciding which cash flows count. The governing word is incremental: compare the world with the project to the world without it, and only the difference matters. Money already spent? Gone in both worlds — ignore it, however much it hurts. A warehouse you own but could rent out? Using it isn't free — charge the project the rent it displaces. Your new product steals sales from your old one? That theft is a real cost of the new product. Capital budgeting is valuation's morality play: count what changes, only what changes, all of what changes.

The incremental commandments

  • Sunk costs are irrelevant. The $5M already spent on research exists in both futures. Deciding based on it is the sunk-cost fallacy with a spreadsheet.
  • Opportunity costs are real. Resources the project consumes that could earn elsewhere (the rentable warehouse, management bandwidth) get charged at their best alternative use.
  • Externalities count, both ways. Cannibalization of existing products is a cost; a halo that lifts other product lines is a benefit.
  • Allocated overhead only if it changes. Corporate HQ's existing rent doesn't belong on the project; a new hire it forces does.
  • Financing stays out. No interest in project cash flows — financing lives in the discount rate (the same separation as FCFF in Chapter 32).

From earnings to free cash flow

$$FCF_t=(\text{Rev}_t-\text{Costs}_t-\text{Dep}_t)(1-t)+\text{Dep}_t-CapEx_t-\Delta NWC_t$$

Depreciation appears twice for one reason: it isn't cash, but it shields cash — each dollar of depreciation saves t dollars of tax (the depreciation tax shield, t × Dep). At the project's end: recover the working capital, and book the after-tax salvage = Sale price − t × (Sale price − Book value) — the taxman shares any gain over book.

Part I · Chapter 08

Valuing Stocks: The Dividend Discount Models

In plain English

What do you, a shareholder, actually receive for owning a stock? Two things only: dividends while you hold it, and the sale price when you sell. But the buyer who pays your sale price is herself only buying future dividends plus her own sale price — and so on forever. Chain the logic and every link's sale price cancels: a stock is worth the present value of all its future dividends. This is the oldest valuation model in the book — the great-grandparent of the full DCF — and its simplicity makes the deep machinery visible.

From one period to forever

$$P_0=\frac{Div_1+P_1}{1+r_e}\;\xrightarrow{\text{chain it}}\;P_0=\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\frac{Div_t}{(1+r_e)^t}\;\xrightarrow{\text{constant }g}\;P_0=\frac{Div_1}{r_e-g}$$

The last form is the Gordon growth model — Chapter 2's growing perpetuity, employed. And where does dividend growth come from? From keeping earnings and reinvesting them well:

$$g=\text{retention rate}\times ROE$$

— the equity-side twin of Chapter 5's g = RR × ROIC. The model immediately teaches the deepest lesson in valuation: cutting the dividend to retain more raises value only if ROE > r_e. Retention at returns below the cost of equity grows the dividend and shrinks the stock — growth without excess returns is worthless (a refrain you will hear again, fortissimo, in Chapter 33).

Modern repairs

Companies increasingly pay out via buybacks, which the per-share DDM misses. The total payout model repairs it: value all dividends plus buybacks for the whole firm, divide by current shares. And for firms that pay nothing at all, the logic generalizes to free cash flow — FCFE is "the dividend the firm could pay," and the enterprise DCF of Part V is the full industrial-strength version. The DDM isn't wrong; it's the special case that taught the general one.

Meridian through the DDM lens

MMD pays out 30% of net income: Div₁ ≈ 0.30 × 1,420/705 = $0.60/share. Retention 70% at sustainable incremental ROE ≈ 11% → g = 0.70 × 11% = 7.7% near-term, fading to 3%. A two-stage DDM (7.7% for 5 years, 3% after) at r_e = 9.10% gives ≈ $31 — below the full DCF's $35.85, mostly because dividends alone miss the buyback capacity the FCFF approach captures. The gap itself is the lesson: choose the cash-flow definition that matches how cash actually reaches owners.

Where people go wrong
  • Applying Gordon directly to a fast grower (g > r_e → nonsense); use multi-stage.
  • Forgetting buybacks — half of modern payout is invisible to the per-share DDM.
  • Mechanically extrapolating g from history instead of deriving it from retention × ROE.

The DDM makes the destruction arithmetic: g rises but P₀ = Div₁/(r−g) falls once you recompute Div₁ and check value per retained dollar.
Each buyer's price is itself the discounted value of what follows her. Substituting P₁'s formula into P₀'s, then P₂'s into that, telescopes the sale price ever further into the discounted future, where its present value → 0. Only the dividend stream survives.

Part II · Chapter 09

Returns and Their Statistics

In plain English

Before measuring how "jumpy" a stock is, we have to agree on what a "return" even means. It sounds trivial — price went from $100 to $105, you made 5% — but tiny definitional choices (simple vs logarithmic, dividend-adjusted or not) decide whether your statistics add up properly. Think of it like agreeing on units before doing physics: get the units wrong and every later calculation is quietly broken.

Two definitions, two superpowers

Simple return: \(R_t=P_t^{adj}/P_{t-1}^{adj}-1\) (always use adjusted prices, which fold dividends and splits back in). Log return: \(r_t=\ln(P_t^{adj}/P_{t-1}^{adj})\).

  • Log returns add across time: the 12-month log return is the sum of the monthly ones. Beautiful for statistics.
  • Simple returns add across assets: a portfolio's simple return is the weighted average of its holdings'. Necessary for portfolio math.
  • Convention for this book: log returns for statistics and regression; simple returns for compounding and portfolios.

Scaling and relating

From monthly data, annualize the mean by ×12 and the volatility by ×√12 (the square-root-of-time rule, valid when returns don't echo themselves month to month). Co-movement between a stock and the market is captured by covariance and its unit-free cousin, correlation:

$$\sigma_{im}=\tfrac{1}{n-1}\sum_t (r_{i,t}-\bar r_i)(r_{m,t}-\bar r_m)\qquad \rho_{im}=\frac{\sigma_{im}}{\sigma_i\sigma_m}$$

Finally, the CAPM (Chapter 13) is a statement about returns above the risk-free rate, so we work with excess returns \(r_t - r_{f,t}\) throughout.

Worked example

Five months of MMD vs SPY log returns (%): MMD {+2.1, −3.0, +4.2, +0.5, −1.8}, SPY {+1.5, −2.0, +2.8, +0.6, −1.4}. Means 0.40% and 0.30%. Covariance = 1.79(%²); variances 2.07 and 0.93; correlation = 1.79/(1.44×0.965) ≈ 0.91 — these two move almost in lockstep, the visual signature of a near-market-beta stock. (Five points is for arithmetic practice; real estimation uses sixty.)

Where people go wrong
  • Unadjusted prices: a dividend looks like a crash; a 2-for-1 split looks like a 50% loss.
  • Mixing frequencies (weekly stock returns vs monthly index returns).
  • Annualizing by √t when returns are autocorrelated — check first.
Check yourself
You end at 75% of where you started: −25%. Log returns show it by addition: ln(1.5) + ln(0.5) = 0.405 − 0.693 = −0.288 = ln(0.75). Simple returns must be compounded, never averaged: the "average of +50 and −50 is zero" intuition is exactly the trap.
Part II · Chapter 10

The Statistics of Evidence: How Not to Fool Yourself

In plain English

Financial data is mostly noise with a whisper of signal, and the human brain is a pattern-finding machine with no off switch — a dangerous combination. Statistics is the discipline of asking, before you believe anything: "could pure luck have produced this?" A fund beat the market three years running — impressive, until you note that among 1,000 coin-flipping funds, about 125 will do that by chance. This chapter builds the toolkit — standard errors, t-statistics, p-values, confidence intervals — that every later chapter's honesty depends on. It is the reason Chapter 12 refuses to trust its own beta and Chapter 16 is so suspicious of discovered "anomalies."

Sampling error: the fog around every estimate

Any average computed from data is an estimate of a truth you can't see, and it comes wrapped in fog. The fog's radius is the standard error:

$$SE(\bar{x})=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$$

Notice the cruelty of the square root: to halve your uncertainty you need four times the data. Markets serve up about 12 monthly observations a year; precision arrives at a geological pace.

The t-statistic: how many fogs from zero?

To test whether an effect is real (say, a fund's alpha), measure its distance from zero in units of standard error:

$$t=\frac{\hat\theta-0}{SE(\hat\theta)}$$

|t| > 2 (roughly) means the estimate sits more than two fogs from zero — unlikely under pure luck (the p-value, the probability of seeing something this extreme if the truth were zero, drops below 5%). The 95% confidence interval \(\hat\theta\pm1.96\,SE\) is the same statement geometrically: the range of truths consistent with your data. Two errors are always on offer: a Type I (believing a fluke) and a Type II (missing a truth); tightening one loosens the other.

The silent killer: multiple testing

Test twenty strategies at the 5% level and expect one "significant" fluke by construction. The quant industry tests millions. This is why discovered patterns demand out-of-sample proof, economic logic, and skepticism proportional to how hard someone searched — the statistical engine behind Chapter 16's warning that data-mined anomalies die in daylight.

Why alpha is nearly unprovable

A manager's true skill is +0.2% per month (a princely 2.4%/yr). Monthly residual noise σ ≈ 4%. To get t = 2 you need \(0.002 = 2\times0.04/\sqrt n\) → \(\sqrt n = 40\) → n = 1,600 months ≈ 133 years. Statistically, a career is not long enough to prove genuine skill — which is why Chapter 12's regression treats its α̂ as noise, and why past performance disclaimers are mathematics, not legal boilerplate.

Where people go wrong
  • Reading "not significant" as "zero" — absence of evidence is mostly absence of data.
  • Reporting the best of many backtests as if it were the only one run.
  • Ignoring that financial returns have fat tails — extreme t-stats arrive more often than the normal curve promises.
Check yourself
No. Among 100 null strategies, the maximum t-stat is expected to exceed 2.5 routinely. Significance thresholds must rise with the number of trials (or the result must survive fresh, untouched data). The search itself manufactured the signal.
Independent noise partially cancels when averaged, but only at the rate √n — each new observation adds less information than the last. Precision in finance is bought at quadratic prices.
Part II · Chapter 11

Portfolio Theory & Diversification

In plain English

"Don't put all your eggs in one basket" turns out to be a theorem. When you split money between two stocks that don't move in perfect lockstep, the portfolio's return is just the average of theirs — but its risk is less than the average, because one's bad days partially offset the other's. That offset is free: diversification is the only genuine free lunch in finance. Follow the logic to the end — hold everything — and the only risk you can't wash out is the market-wide kind. That last step is why beta (next chapter) is the only risk that earns a premium.

The math of the free lunch

For two assets with weights w₁, w₂:

$$E[R_p]=w_1\mu_1+w_2\mu_2\qquad \sigma_p^2=w_1^2\sigma_1^2+w_2^2\sigma_2^2+2w_1w_2\,\rho_{12}\,\sigma_1\sigma_2$$

The return is linear in weights; the risk is not — and the entire benefit hides in ρ. At ρ = 1, σ_p is just the weighted average (no lunch). At any ρ < 1, the cross-term shrinks and risk falls below the average while return doesn't. At ρ = −1, risk can be eliminated entirely.

Plot every possible mix of many assets in risk–return space and the upper-left boundary is the efficient frontier: the menu of best-possible portfolios. Add a risk-free asset and one portfolio on the frontier becomes special — the tangency portfolio, which maximizes the Sharpe ratio:

$$\text{Sharpe}=\frac{E[R]-r_f}{\sigma}$$

Every investor then holds the same tangency portfolio in some mix with cash — the capital market line — differing only in dosage. In CAPM equilibrium, that tangency portfolio is the market portfolio. The bridge to Chapter 12 is now visible.

What diversification cannot remove

With N equally weighted assets, portfolio variance → the average covariance as N grows: the individual variances wash out; the co-movement does not. Firm-specific risk (recalls, scandals, lucky quarters) diversifies to zero; market-wide risk (recessions, rate shocks) survives in every portfolio. Hence the punchline the whole part is built on: only covariance with the market — beta — can command compensation, because everything else is removable for free.

Two stocks, one lunch

Stock A: μ 10%, σ 25%. Stock B: μ 8%, σ 20%. ρ = 0.3. A 50/50 mix: E[R_p] = 9%. Risk: σ_p² = 0.25×0.0625 + 0.25×0.04 + 2×0.25×0.3×0.25×0.20 = 0.01563 + 0.01 + 0.0075 = 0.03313 → σ_p = 18.2% — below either stock's risk, and well below the 22.5% naive average, while the return is exactly the average. The 4.3-point risk discount cost nothing. That is the lunch.

If its correlation with the existing holdings is low (or negative), the cross-term it contributes shrinks total variance by more than its own variance adds at modest weights. Risk lives in the covariance matrix, not in any asset alone.

Part II · Chapter 12

Beta by Regression: OLS Done Properly

In plain English

Some of a stock's bouncing is private drama (a recall, a CEO scandal) and some is the whole market's tide. Diversified investors can wash away the private drama by holding many stocks — so the market only pays them for the tide-exposure. Beta measures that tide-exposure: beta 1 bobs with the market, beta 2 swings twice as hard, beta 0.5 half as hard. We estimate it by drawing the best straight line through a scatter plot of "stock's monthly return vs market's monthly return" — that line's slope is beta, and the line-drawing method is ordinary least squares (OLS).

The regression

$$r_{i,t}-r_{f,t}=\alpha+\beta\,(r_{m,t}-r_{f,t})+\varepsilon_t \qquad \hat\beta=\frac{Cov(r_i^{ex},r_m^{ex})}{Var(r_m^{ex})}=\rho_{im}\frac{\sigma_i}{\sigma_m}$$

The estimate comes with honesty bars: the standard error. With 60 monthly observations, SE(β̂) is typically 0.15–0.25, so a point estimate of 1.10 is statistically indistinguishable from 0.85 or 1.35. Never treat a regression beta as a precise number. R² tells you what share of the stock's variance is market tide (0.25–0.45 is normal); the rest is private drama.

Diagnostics — what separates a student regression from a professional one

  1. Heteroskedasticity (volatility clusters in calm and stormy regimes): point estimate fine, error bars wrong → use White robust standard errors.
  2. Autocorrelation (residuals echo): Durbin–Watson ≈ 2 means clean → otherwise Newey–West errors.
  3. Structural breaks (the company changed — big acquisition, divestiture): Chow test, or a rolling-beta plot; estimate on the post-break sample.
  4. Outliers: one crash month can own the slope; inspect influence, winsorize extremes with disclosure.

Two repairs for a noisy beta

Blume adjustment (betas drift toward 1 as firms mature): \(\beta_{adj}=\tfrac{2}{3}\hat\beta+\tfrac{1}{3}\). Bottom-up beta — the defensible workhorse: take several pure-play peers' betas, strip out each one's leverage (un-lever), average the clean "business risk" betas, then re-dress the average in your firm's own leverage:

$$\beta_U=\frac{\beta_L}{1+(1-t)\frac{D}{E}}\qquad\qquad \beta_L=\beta_U\Big[1+(1-t)\frac{D}{E}\Big]$$

Averaging across N peers shrinks estimation noise roughly by √N — which is why bottom-up usually beats your own noisy history.

Meridian's beta, both ways

Regression: 60 months → β̂ = 0.95, robust SE = 0.21, R² = 0.38, α insignificant. The 95% confidence interval is a sobering [0.54, 1.36].

Bottom-up: five medtech peers, betas {0.85, 1.05, 0.90, 1.10, 0.95} with D/E {0.30, 0.20, 0.40, 0.25, 0.35}, t = 22%. Unlevered: {0.69, 0.91, 0.69, 0.92, 0.75} → average β_U = 0.79. Re-lever at MMD's market D/E of 0.314: β_L = 0.79 × (1 + 0.78 × 0.314) = 0.98.

The two methods agree (0.95 vs 0.98) — we adopt β = 0.98 from the more stable bottom-up route and let the regression serve as confirmation. When they disagree, hunt for the structural break first.

Where people go wrong
  • Regressing raw instead of excess returns.
  • Choosing the estimation window after seeing which beta you prefer.
  • Treating a "significant α" at monthly frequency as tradable skill — it almost never is.
  • Using book D/E inside Hamada — the formula wants market values.
Check yourself
Debtholders take a fixed slice off the top, so whatever the business's fortunes, equity holders absorb the swings on a thinner cushion — the same business risk concentrated on fewer dollars. Hamada's formula is just that concentration written down.
That is a structural break: the pre-acquisition months describe a firm that no longer exists. Estimate on post-deal data (too short to be reliable) or — better — build a bottom-up beta from peers of the combined business mix.

CAPM & the Cost of Equity

In plain English

Suppose your friend asks you to invest in her business instead of leaving your money in safe government bonds paying 4.2%. What return would make that worth your while? You'd want the 4.2% you're giving up, plus extra pay for the risk — and logically, twice the risk should earn twice the extra pay. That sentence is the CAPM: required return = risk-free rate + (how risky, in betas) × (the going price of one unit of risk). The answer it produces — the cost of equity — is what shareholders silently charge the company for their capital.

$$k_e=r_f+\beta\times ERP$$

Each ingredient, chosen carefully

Risk-free rate. Use a default-free government yield whose maturity matches the duration of the cash flows being discounted. Equity cash flows are long-lived — a typical DCF has a duration of 8–15 years (a fixed-income concept doing equity work; see Chapter 20) — so the 10-year Treasury is standard. Never the 3-month bill for valuing something perpetual.

Equity risk premium (ERP) — the expected extra return of the whole stock market over the risk-free rate, i.e., the going price of one beta of risk. Three estimation schools: historical (long-run realized average, ~4.5–7% depending on method — backward-looking, ±2% standard error even with a century of data); implied (solve for the rate that makes today's index level equal the present value of expected market cash flows — an IRR on the whole market, typically 4–5.5%, internally consistent with current prices; this book's default); survey (what CFOs and academics say). Pick one, state it, use it for every firm in the analysis.

Extensions: a small-cap premium (contested; irrelevant for large caps), country risk premia for emerging-market cash flows, and the Fama–French factor models as a diagnostic — if your stock has heavy size/value factor loadings, plain CAPM may be mis-scaled.

Meridian's cost of equity

r_f = 4.20% (10-year Treasury), ERP = 5.0% (implied), β = 0.98 (Chapter 12).

$$k_e = 4.20\% + 0.98\times5.0\% = \mathbf{9.10\%}$$

Honesty bar: across the regression beta's confidence interval [0.54, 1.36], k_e spans 6.9%–11.0%. Chapter 36 will refuse to ignore that spread — it becomes a probability distribution on the discount rate inside the Monte Carlo.

Part II · Chapter 14

Arbitrage Pricing Theory: Many Tides, One Law

In plain English

CAPM says stocks ride one tide — the market. Look closely and you see several distinct currents: surprise inflation, growth shocks, swings in interest rates, credit fear. The Arbitrage Pricing Theory (Ross, 1976) says: fine — let returns be driven by several factors, each with its own price of risk. Its genius is what it does not assume. No claims about investor psychology, no requirement that everyone hold the market portfolio. Just one iron rule — the Law of One Price again: if expected returns ever drifted away from what factor exposures justify, traders could build a zero-cost, zero-risk portfolio that prints money, and its own exploitation would close the gap.

The model

Returns are generated by K systematic factors plus diversifiable noise:

$$R_i=E[R_i]+\beta_{i1}F_1+\beta_{i2}F_2+\cdots+\beta_{iK}F_K+\varepsilon_i$$

where each \(F_k\) is a factor's surprise (realization minus expectation — only news moves prices). No-arbitrage then forces expected returns to be linear in the exposures:

$$E[R_i]=r_f+\beta_{i1}\lambda_1+\beta_{i2}\lambda_2+\cdots+\beta_{iK}\lambda_K$$

with \(\lambda_k\) the risk premium per unit of factor-k exposure. Why must it be linear? Because exposures can be mixed like ingredients: combine assets to replicate any target's betas exactly; if the replica and the target promised different returns, short one, buy the other — free money with zero net risk. Diversification kills the ε's; arbitrage kills the mispricing.

What are the factors?

APT is deliberately silent — a freedom and a burden. The classic macro implementation (Chen, Roll & Ross) used surprises in industrial production, inflation, the term spread (long minus short rates) and the default spread (low-grade minus high-grade yields). The statistical route extracts factors from the return data itself (principal components). And the characteristics route — sorting stocks by size, value, profitability — leads directly to the next chapter. CAPM turns out to be the one-factor special case; APT is the genus, CAPM a species.

A two-factor expected return

Suppose the priced factors are market risk (λ = 5%) and inflation-surprise risk (λ = −1.5%; assets that suffer when inflation spikes must pay extra, assets that hedge it accept less). MMD: β_mkt = 0.98, β_infl = +0.20 (medtech pricing is somewhat inflation-protected). With r_f = 4.2%:

$$E[R]=4.2\%+0.98(5\%)+0.20(-1.5\%)=4.2\%+4.9\%-0.3\%=\mathbf{8.8\%}$$

— a touch below the CAPM's 9.10%, the discount earned by MMD's modest inflation hedge. Two views of one cost of equity; the gap is itself information.

Where people go wrong
  • Treating factor realizations (not surprises) as the F's — expected components are already in the price.
  • Harvesting "premia" from factors with no economic reason to be priced — Chapter 10's multiple-testing trap at industrial scale.
  • Forgetting APT's promise is approximate for well-diversified portfolios, not exact for single stocks.
Check yourself
Short $X of the 9% portfolio, buy $X of the 11% one. Factor exposures cancel exactly; diversification has already killed idiosyncratic risk; net outlay zero — yet the position earns 2% of X with certainty. Arbitrageurs scale it until buying/selling pressure equalizes the two expected returns. That enforcement is the pricing equation.
Set K = 1 and let the single factor be the market portfolio's surprise: the APT equation becomes E[R] = r_f + β·λ_mkt, and λ_mkt = E[R_m] − r_f. CAPM adds equilibrium reasoning about why the market is the factor; APT needs only no-arbitrage.
Part II · Chapter 15

Fama–French: Three Factors, Then Five — and Judging Performance

In plain English

In the early 1990s, Fama and French ran a brutal experiment: sort every stock by size, and separately by cheapness (book value ÷ market value), and check whether beta explains the return differences. It didn't. Small stocks beat big ones; cheap stocks beat glamorous ones — persistently, beyond anything beta could justify. Rather than abandon factor pricing, they expanded it: build portfolios that isolate those effects and treat them as new risk factors. Three factors became the industry's lens; in 2015 they added two more — profitability and investment — making five. Whatever a fund manager's "skill" is, it must now be measured after stripping out what these mechanical styles deliver for free.

The three-factor model

$$R_i-r_f=\alpha_i+\beta_{mkt}(R_m-r_f)+\beta_s\,SMB+\beta_v\,HML+\varepsilon_i$$

  • SMB ("small minus big") — the return of a portfolio long small-cap stocks, short large-caps.
  • HML ("high minus low") — long high book-to-market (value) stocks, short low (growth/glamour).

The factors are built as long–short portfolios, so each is an investable bet whose average return is the premium itself. Carhart later added UMD (momentum: recent winners minus losers) as a fourth.

The five-factor model (2015)

$$R_i-r_f=\alpha_i+\beta_{mkt}MKT+\beta_s SMB+\beta_v HML+\beta_r RMW+\beta_c CMA+\varepsilon_i$$

  • RMW ("robust minus weak") — long high-profitability firms, short low. Profitable firms earn more than their prices suggest.
  • CMA ("conservative minus aggressive") — long low-investment firms, short empire-builders. Heavy asset growth predicts poor returns.

Both additions flow from the dividend-discount logic of Chapter 8: holding price fixed, higher expected profitability ⇒ higher expected return, and higher required reinvestment ⇒ lower. The model is theory-tethered, not just data-mined. The unending debate: are these premia risk (value firms are distress-fragile; they crash together in bad times) or mispricing (investors overpay for glamour)? Decades in, the honest answer remains: some of each — and for measurement purposes it barely matters.

Judging performance: the alpha hierarchy

The intercept α is what remains after the factors take their share — the only defensible definition of skill. The scoreboard family:

  • Jensen's alpha — the intercept vs CAPM alone: return unexplained by market exposure.
  • Treynor ratio \(=(\bar R-r_f)/\beta\) — excess return per unit of systematic risk; right for judging one sleeve inside a diversified whole.
  • Sharpe ratio (Chapter 11) — excess return per unit of total risk; right for a standalone portfolio.
  • Information ratio \(=\alpha/\sigma(\varepsilon)\) — alpha per unit of the tracking risk taken to get it; the active manager's true exam grade.
The disappearing alpha

A fund returns 11.5%/yr vs r_f 4.2%. CAPM regression: β = 0.9, so expected = 4.2 + 0.9×5.0 = 8.7% → Jensen's α = +2.8%/yr. A star! Now the five-factor regression: loadings β_v = 0.6 on HML (premium ≈ 3%) and β_r = 0.4 on RMW (premium ≈ 2.5%) → styles explain 0.6×3 + 0.4×2.5 = 2.8%. Five-factor α = 0.0%. The manager wasn't picking stocks; he was (perhaps unknowingly) running a value-and-quality tilt you can buy in an index fund for 5 basis points. Factor models exist to send exactly this invoice.

For your valuation work: run MMD's returns through the five-factor model as a diagnostic. Heavy loadings on HML or RMW mean the plain CAPM k_e of Chapter 13 may be mis-scaled — and the honest response is a sensitivity band on the discount rate, fed into Chapter 36's simulation.

Where people go wrong
  • Crowning managers on CAPM alpha when a style tilt explains everything.
  • Stacking every published factor (hundreds exist) — Chapter 10's multiple-testing alarm should be deafening.
  • Forgetting factor premia arrive with crashes: value's rewards include value's winters.
Check yourself
Long–short isolates the effect and nets out the market: SMB moves only with the size effect, not with overall market direction. It is simultaneously a clean measurement instrument and an investable strategy whose mean return is the premium.
Information ratios: 0.20 vs 0.50 — the second, decisively. Scaled to equal active risk, the second manager's process generates 2.5× the alpha. IR measures the quality of the machine, not the size of one bet.

Market Efficiency & Investor Behavior

In plain English

If thousands of smart, motivated analysts are all hunting for mispriced stocks, their very hunting drives prices toward fair value — the hunters eat the prey until little prey remains. That is the efficient markets hypothesis: prices already reflect available information, so beating the market consistently is extraordinarily hard. It is not a law of physics — humans panic, herd, and overreach, and real anomalies exist — but it is the right null hypothesis. For a valuation practitioner the lesson is humility with teeth: your model's disagreement with the price is a claim that you know something the marginal investor doesn't — so name it, or fold.

Three strengths of the claim

  • Weak form: prices reflect past prices — patterns and charts alone shouldn't work.
  • Semi-strong form: prices reflect all public information — earnings, filings, news are absorbed almost instantly; reading the 10-K faster doesn't help, reading it better might.
  • Strong form: prices reflect even private information — contradicted by the profitability of (illegal) insider trading; nobody believes this one.

Empirical reality is a spectrum, not a verdict. Large-cap prices are very efficient; small, neglected, hard-to-short corners less so. And Chapter 12's regression already showed the microfoundation: estimated alphas hover near zero with wide error bars — exactly what a competitive market predicts.

The cracks: anomalies and behavior

Decades of data show persistent patterns CAPM can't explain: momentum (recent winners keep winning ~6–12 months), the value premium (cheap-on-fundamentals stocks outperform — the HML factor from Chapter 13's Fama–French discussion), post-earnings drift. Behavioral finance supplies suspects: overconfidence, anchoring, the disposition effect (selling winners, clutching losers), herding. The deep question is whether anomalies are mispricing or hidden risk premia — and the honest answer is "some of each." What keeps them alive either way is limits to arbitrage: shorting is costly, capital is impatient, and "the market can stay irrational longer than you can stay solvent."

What this means for your model

Efficiency is why the reverse DCF (Chapter 35) is this book's most decision-useful tool: it treats the price as the aggregated forecast of everyone else, extracts that forecast, and asks where — specifically — you disagree and why your information or judgment is better. "The market is wrong" is not an analysis; "the market is pricing 8.2% growth because it extrapolates the current device cycle, and the clinical-trial calendar says the cycle ends in 2027" is.

Where people go wrong
  • Treating EMH as either gospel (then why value anything?) or refuted (then why is alpha so rare?). It is a baseline with measurable cracks.
  • Backtesting until something "works" — data-mined anomalies die in daylight.
  • Confusing a risk premium for skill: value and momentum returns come with crashes attached.
Check yourself
Grossman–Stiglitz logic: prices become efficient precisely through the trading of informed analysts, who must earn just enough to cover the cost of becoming informed. Equilibrium is near-efficiency — enough inefficiency to pay the police, not enough to enrich bystanders.

Volatility Dynamics & Risk Measures: GARCH, VaR, Expected Shortfall

In plain English

Watch any market chart and you'll see weather, not static: long calm stretches, then storms that cluster — turbulent days follow turbulent days. So "the" volatility is a fiction; volatility has moods, and modeling the mood is the GARCH family's job: tomorrow's storminess is a blend of today's shock and today's storminess. Then comes the boardroom question every risk model must answer: "how much could we lose?" Value-at-Risk answers it as a sentence anyone can repeat — "95% of days, we lose less than $X" — and Expected Shortfall repairs VaR's famous blind spot by asking what happens on the days beyond the line.

GARCH(1,1): volatility with a memory

$$\sigma_t^2=\omega+\alpha\,\varepsilon_{t-1}^2+\beta\,\sigma_{t-1}^2$$

Read it aloud: today's variance = a base level (ω) + a reaction to yesterday's surprise (α, typically ≈ 0.08) + persistence of yesterday's variance (β, typically ≈ 0.90). With α + β ≈ 0.98, shocks decay slowly — the clustering, captured. Long-run variance is \(\omega/(1-\alpha-\beta)\), and forecasts glide from current conditions back toward it. (The simpler EWMA used by RiskMetrics is the ω = 0, α + β = 1 special case.) GARCH forecasts feed three places in this book: honest Monte Carlo inputs (Chapter 36), comparison with the option market's implied σ (Chapter 26), and the VaR below.

Value-at-Risk and its repair

Parametric (normal) one-day VaR at confidence level 1−p for a position of size W:

$$VaR_{p}=z_{p}\,\sigma\,W\qquad(z_{5\%}=1.645,\;z_{1\%}=2.326)$$

Three estimation roads: parametric (assume a distribution — fragile in fat-tailed markets), historical simulation (replay the last 500 days on today's portfolio — assumption-light, sample-bound), Monte Carlo (simulate from a fitted model such as GARCH). VaR's blind spot is structural: it is a threshold, utterly silent about how bad the bad 5% is — two books can share a VaR while one risks 1.1× it and the other 20×. Expected Shortfall (CVaR) repairs this by averaging the tail:

$$ES_p=E[\,\text{loss}\mid \text{loss}>VaR_p\,]\qquad\text{normal case: }ES_p=\frac{\phi(z_p)}{p}\,\sigma\,W$$

ES is also coherent (diversification can never increase it — a property VaR fails), which is why regulators migrated to it.

A $1M position in MMD

GARCH says current daily σ = 1.5%. 95% one-day VaR = 1.645 × 1.5% × 1,000,000 = $24,700. Expected shortfall: ES = φ(1.645)/0.05 × σ × W = (0.1031/0.05) × 15,000 = 2.06 × 15,000 = $30,900 — the average bad-day-beyond-the-line loss, a fifth worse than the line itself. After a shock day of ε = −4%: σ²_{t+1} = ω + 0.08(0.04)² + 0.90(0.015)² → σ ≈ 1.82% → VaR jumps ~21% overnight. Risk is a forecast, not a constant.

Part III · Chapter 18

Bond Pricing Fundamentals

In plain English

A bond is an IOU with a printed schedule: "I will pay you $20 every six months, and $1,000 back in 2034." Since the payments are fixed, the only question is what that schedule is worth today — pure discounting. And here is the seesaw at the heart of all bond math: when interest rates rise, old bonds fall in price. Why? Your old bond pays $20 per period forever-fixed; if new bonds now pay $25, nobody buys yours at full price — its price must drop until its overall return matches the new world.

The pricing formula and its inverse

$$P=\sum_{t=1}^{2T}\frac{C/2}{(1+y/2)^t}+\frac{F}{(1+y/2)^{2T}}$$

(US bonds pay semiannually: halve the coupon and yield, double the periods.) Run it forward and a yield gives a price; run it backward and a market price gives the yield to maturity (YTM) — the bond's internal rate of return, found numerically. Four relationships to internalize:

  1. Price and yield move inversely (the seesaw).
  2. Coupon = yield ⇔ price = par (100). Below yield → discount; above → premium.
  3. Longer maturity → bigger price swings per unit of yield (formalized as duration, Chapter 20).
  4. The price–yield curve bows toward you (convexity): prices gain more from a fall in yields than they lose from an equal rise.

Market plumbing: quoted "clean" prices exclude accrued interest (the seller's earned share of the next coupon); buyers pay clean + accrued = "dirty." Day-count conventions (30/360 corporates, Actual/Actual Treasuries) decide the accrued math — match your course's convention when replicating homework.

Solve for the y making 16 payments of $20 plus $1,000 worth $930: at y = 5.0% the PV is 934.7 (too high), at 5.1% it is 928.6 — interpolating, YTM ≈ 5.07%. That number is the market's live quote of MMD's 8-year borrowing cost — far better evidence than anything in the accounting statements, and a direct input to Chapter 22.

The Yield Curve & Bootstrapping

In plain English

Banks don't charge the same rate for a 1-year loan and a 30-year loan — and neither does the bond market. The yield curve is the full menu: a rate for every horizon. A single bond's YTM blurs this menu into one average number; the curve un-blurs it, assigning each future date its own pure rate (the spot rate). "Bootstrapping" is the trick for recovering those pure rates one maturity at a time from ordinary coupon bonds — like solving a staircase of equations where each step uses the steps below it.

Spot rates and the no-arbitrage principle

The spot rate \(z_t\) is the yield of a zero-coupon bond maturing at t; its discount factor is \(d_t=(1+z_t)^{-t}\). Any bond is just a bundle of zeros, so its fair price is \(P=\sum_t CF_t\,d_t\). If the traded price differed, you could profit risklessly by splitting the bond into its pieces or reassembling them — which is why the spot curve is the curve for discounting any fixed cash-flow stream, a firm's whole debt stack included.

Bootstrapping, step by step

Given par yields (coupon bonds priced at 100): the 1-year bond has one cash flow, so z₁ = its yield. The 2-year bond's price equation has only z₂ unknown once z₁ is known — solve it. Each maturity adds exactly one new unknown. Forward rates then fall out of the spots: the rate the curve implies for borrowing between two future dates,

$$(1+z_{t_2})^{t_2}=(1+z_{t_1})^{t_1}(1+f_{t_1,t_2})^{t_2-t_1}$$

Forwards are break-even reinvestment rates — and the correct basis for forecasting a company's floating-rate interest expense (used in Chapter 31's debt schedule). Why does the curve usually slope up? Expectations (forwards ≈ expected future short rates) plus a term premium (extra pay for bearing duration risk); an inverted curve means expected future rates sit below today's — historically a recession flag.

Bootstrapping a 3-year curve

Par yields: 1y 4.00% · 2y 4.50% · 3y 4.80% (annual pay).

z₁ = 4.000%. For z₂: 100 = 4.5/1.04 + 104.5/(1+z₂)² → (1+z₂)² = 104.5/95.673 = 1.09226 → z₂ = 4.511%. For z₃: 100 − 4.615 − 4.394 = 90.991 = 104.8/(1+z₃)³ → z₃ = 4.823%. (Spots sit a hair above par yields on an upward curve — the coupon effect.)

Implied 1-year rate starting in year 2: f₂,₃ = 1.15176/1.09226 − 1 = 5.45% — the market's break-even for short rates two years out.

Duration & Convexity: Interest-Rate Risk

In plain English

Picture a bond's cash flows as weights sitting along a timeline see-saw. Duration is the balance point — the average wait, in years, for your money (each payment weighted by its present value). The magic is that this same number doubles as a risk gauge: a duration of 7 means "if rates move 1%, this bond's price moves about 7% the other way." Long wait = big swing. Convexity is the curvature correction for bigger moves — and it works in your favor: prices rise more when rates fall than they drop when rates rise.

The math

$$D_{Mac}=\frac{\sum_t t\cdot\frac{CF_t}{(1+y)^t}}{P}\qquad D_{mod}=\frac{D_{Mac}}{1+y}\qquad \frac{\Delta P}{P}\approx -D_{mod}\,\Delta y+\tfrac{1}{2}C(\Delta y)^2$$

Rules of thumb: duration rises with maturity, falls with coupon and yield; a zero-coupon bond's Macaulay duration equals its maturity (you wait the whole time for everything). A portfolio's duration is the value-weighted average of its bonds' — which is how we measure the rate-risk of MMD's entire debt stack at once.

Worked example — and a punchline for equities

A 3-year, 5% annual bond at y = 5% prices at par. The PV-weighted times sum to 285.94, so D_Mac = 2.859 years, D_mod = 2.859/1.05 = 2.723. A 100 bp yield rise predicts −2.72%; exact repricing gives −2.67% — convexity (≈ 10) repairs the 5 bp gap via +½ × 10 × 0.01² = +0.05%.

The punchline: compute the same statistic on MMD's forecast FCFF stream with WACC as the yield, and you get ≈ 11 years. That is the rigorous answer to "why the 10-year Treasury in CAPM?" from Chapter 13 — match the risk-free tenor to the duration of what you're discounting. Fixed-income mathematics quietly governs equity valuation too.

Part III · Chapter 21

Immunization, Key Rates & Interest-Rate Swaps

In plain English

Chapter 20 measured rate risk; this chapter manages it. A pension fund owes $100M in ten years and holds bonds against the promise. Rates move — and something lovely happens if the assets' duration matches the liability's: a rate rise cuts bond prices but lets coupons reinvest richer; a rate fall does the reverse. Match durations and the two effects cancel: the promise is immunized. And when a treasurer wants to change rate exposure without trading a single bond, the interest-rate swap — finance's most traded contract — swaps fixed payments for floating ones with a handshake.

Immunization: the three conditions

  1. PV of assets = PV of liabilities (funded);
  2. Duration of assets = duration of liabilities (first-order matched);
  3. Convexity of assets ≥ convexity of liabilities (curvature works for you on big moves).

Caveats that keep ALM desks employed: immunization holds for parallel shifts and drifts as time passes and rates move — it must be rebalanced. Real curves twist, which is where key-rate durations come in: sensitivity to a bump at each maturity point separately. They always sum to the ordinary duration, but they reveal whether a portfolio that "matches at 10 years" is secretly a 2-year/30-year barbell betting hard on the curve's shape.

Floating-rate notes and the swap

A floating-rate note resets its coupon to the market rate each period — so at every reset it is worth ≈ par: near-zero duration, the fixed-coupon bond's mirror twin. A plain-vanilla interest-rate swap exchanges fixed payments for floating on a notional principal. Decompose it and the pricing is already in your hands:

$$V_{\text{swap, pay-floating}}=B_{\text{fixed}}-B_{\text{float}}$$

— long a fixed bond, short a floater (or vice versa). The swap rate is set so the swap starts at zero value: it is the coupon making a par bond on today's curve (Chapter 19's bootstrapping, employed). Duration of the position = duration(fixed leg) − duration(float leg ≈ 0): a receive-fixed swap adds duration; pay-fixed removes it — exposure surgery with no bonds traded.

Building the barbell — and MMD's hedge

Immunize a 10-year-duration liability using a 2-year bond (D = 1.9) and a 20-year bond (D = 12.6): solve w(1.9) + (1−w)(12.6) = 10 → w = 2.6/10.7 = 24.3% short / 75.7% long. Key-rate check: this barbell matches total duration but is exposed to a steepening twist — flag it, size it, accept or hedge it.

MMD's treasury: 280 of floating lease/revolver exposure becomes fixed via a pay-fixed swap — the forecast's interest line (Chapter 31) then prices off the swap rate instead of the floating forwards, and earnings volatility falls. Same firm, same debts, different rate exposure: the swap is the dial.

Where people go wrong
  • Immunizing once and walking away — duration drifts daily; rebalancing is the strategy.
  • Matching total duration while mismatching key rates — solvent on parallel shifts, broke on a twist.
  • Forgetting a floater's duration is the time to the next reset, not to maturity.
Check yourself
Pay-fixed = short the fixed bond + long the floater. Shorting the high-duration leg subtracts its duration; the floater adds ≈ nothing. Net: the firm's liabilities behave more like floating debt — value-stable when rates move, cash-flow-variable instead.
Fine today — price loss and reinvestment gain offset. But the move changed both durations unequally, and time will keep changing them. Immunization is a feedback control loop, not a one-time vaccine.

Credit Risk & the Cost of Debt

In plain English

Lending to a company is lending to the government plus accepting a chance of not being paid back. The extra yield a company pays over Treasuries — the credit spread — is the price of that chance. So a company's true borrowing cost is never a number you invent: it's risk-free rate + spread, and the spread can be read three ways — straight off its traded bonds, off its credit rating, or off its accounting health. A careful analyst reads all three and checks they agree.

What's inside a spread

$$\text{spread}\approx PD\times LGD+\text{risk premium}$$

PD is the annual probability of default; LGD the loss if it happens (1 − recovery; senior unsecured bonds historically recover ~40%, so LGD ≈ 60%). A 110 bp spread therefore implies a break-even default probability of about 1.1%/0.6 ≈ 1.8% per year before risk premium — a sanity check that catches inconsistent assumptions fast.

Three routes to the cost of debt

  1. Market route (best when bonds trade): YTM of each outstanding issue (Chapter 18), weighted by market value.
  2. Synthetic-rating route: interest coverage (Chapter 5) → implied rating from a published lookup → that rating's current spread + matched Treasury. Essential for private firms; confirmation for public ones.
  3. Book route (smell test only): interest expense ÷ average debt — backward-looking, embeds stale coupons.

Interest is tax-deductible, so the WACC uses the after-tax cost \(k_d(1-t)\) — using the marginal tax rate, and only to the extent the firm has profits to shield. Finally, for the bridge in Chapter 34, debt enters at market value: reprice every issue on today's curve + spread. When rates have risen since issuance, market value < book — those old cheap coupons are quietly an asset to shareholders.

  • Using the coupon rate as the cost of debt — that is history, not today's price of risk.
  • Book debt in WACC weights and the bridge while market value has drifted.
  • Applying the tax shield to a firm with loss carryforwards that pays no cash tax.
  • Ignoring leases — they are debt; capitalize them.

Capital Structure: From Modigliani–Miller to the Real World

In plain English

Imagine a pizza. Does cutting it into more slices give you more pizza? Modigliani and Miller's famous answer: no — in a perfect world, how you slice a company's cash flows between lenders and owners cannot change the total value of the company. So why do real CFOs agonize over debt? Because the real world taxes the slices differently (interest is tax-deductible — the government subsidizes the debt slice), and too much debt risks dropping the whole pizza (bankruptcy is expensive and scares away customers and employees). Real capital structure is the balance between that subsidy and that danger.

The frictionless benchmark (MM)

Proposition I: with no taxes or frictions, \(V_L=V_U\) — financing mix is irrelevant, because investors can lever or unlever on their own ("homemade leverage"). Proposition II: leverage raises the expected return on equity exactly in proportion to the added risk:

$$k_e=k_U+(k_U-k_d)\frac{D}{E}$$

so the blended WACC stays flat. Debt doesn't create value here; it redistributes risk. (Notice this is the same statement as Hamada's beta relevering in Chapter 12 — one written in returns, the other in risk. They are the same physics.)

Adding reality, one friction at a time

Taxes: interest deductibility makes debt subsidized; for permanent debt, \(V_L=V_U+tD\). Taken literally that says "borrow infinitely" — which firms don't, so something is missing. Trade-off theory supplies it:

$$V_L=V_U+PV(\text{tax shields})-PV(\text{expected distress costs})$$

Distress costs are direct (lawyers, ~3–5% of value) and — much larger — indirect: customers won't buy pacemakers from a maker that might not exist to support them; talent leaves; assets are fire-sold; good projects go unfunded. Since the probability of distress rises steeply with leverage, there is an interior optimum: an industry-specific sweet spot. Stable, hard-asset businesses (utilities) carry lots of debt; volatile, intangible ones (biotech) carry little.

Pecking order theory adds information: managers know more than investors, so issuing stock signals "we think it's overpriced" and tanks the price. Firms therefore finance internally first, then debt, then equity last — which explains the puzzle that the most profitable firms often carry the least debt (they never needed any). Agency effects cut both ways: debt disciplines managers flush with cash, but heavy debt causes underinvestment and risk-shifting.

How a modeler uses all this: trade-off logic supplies the target structure (≈ industry norm adjusted for the firm's volatility); pecking order says how fast the firm drifts toward it; and MM II supplies the consistency law — change D/E in a scenario, and β and k_e must move with it. They are not free parameters.

Meridian's leverage, judged

MMD's market D/V = 8,800/36,800 ≈ 24% — right at the medtech peer norm of 20–25%, so current ≈ target and WACC can use current market weights. Trade-off sketch: at 24% leverage, tax-shield value ≈ 0.22 × 8,800 ≈ 1,940 against expected distress of only ≈ 210 (A− rating, low probability). Pushing to 50% leverage roughly doubles the shield but drops the rating toward BB, multiplying distress probability ~7× — the marginal benefit evaporates. MMD's sweet spot plausibly spans 20–35%: precision impossible, direction unmistakable.

Where people go wrong
  • Treating V = V_U + tD as exact — it assumes permanent debt and a fully usable shield.
  • Doubling leverage in a scenario while holding k_e fixed — violates MM II.
  • Confusing book leverage (an accounting residue) with the market leverage the theory describes.
Check yourself
Because each extra dollar of debt makes the remaining equity riskier — MM II raises k_e exactly enough to offset the cheap debt. WACC only truly falls because of the tax subsidy, and only until distress costs start eating the benefit.
Pecking order: it funds everything internally and never reaches debt in the hierarchy. Plus trade-off: intangible-asset businesses have catastrophic indirect distress costs and volatile cash flows, so their optimum leverage is low anyway.

Assembling the WACC

In plain English

A company's capital comes from two faucets: lenders (who charge ~5%) and shareholders (who silently require ~9%). The WACC is just the blended price of the mix — like the average price of a fruit punch made from two juices, weighted by how much of each is in the bowl. It is the single most consequential number in valuation because every future dollar gets divided by it, compounded. We built each ingredient in earlier chapters; this chapter only mixes — but mixing is where most real-world errors live.

$$WACC=\frac{E}{V}k_e+\frac{D}{V}k_d(1-t)$$

The four laws of assembly

  1. Market values only. E = fully diluted shares × price; D = market value of debt including leases (Chapter 22). Book equity can be off by a factor of 3–10.
  2. Current vs target weights. At the target (MMD is), use current market weights; away from it, use target weights — and know you've embedded a migration assumption.
  3. The consistency triangle: β, k_e, and the weights must all describe the same capital structure. Relever beta at target D/E? Then weight at target D/V too.
  4. The circularity: equity value depends on WACC, whose weights depend on equity value. For public firms, use the market E (you are testing the market's price, not your own output); for private firms, iterate 3–5 rounds to convergence; or fix target weights, which breaks the loop by assumption.

Part IV · Chapter 25

Payout Policy & APV

In plain English

When a company hands cash back — dividends or share buybacks — does that create value? MM's answer again: not by itself; a dividend just moves value from the share price into your pocket, like withdrawing from your own ATM. What does matter: taxes, the powerful signal a dividend sends ("we are confident enough to commit"), the discipline of taking idle cash away from empire-building managers, and — crucially for buybacks — the price paid. A buyback below intrinsic value transfers wealth from sellers to remaining holders; above it, the reverse. Your own DCF is exactly the tool that says which is happening.

Buyback arithmetic, demystified

Buybacks mechanically raise EPS whenever the earnings yield exceeds the return idle cash was earning — but EPS accretion is not value creation. Value is created only by buying below intrinsic value. MMD buying stock at $40 when our model says $35.85 would be (mildly) destroying value per remaining share; at $29 it would be building it. Payout policy also drives the model's mechanics: the cash/buyback line of the forecast and the share-count path used for per-share value in Chapter 34.

APV — valuation for changing capital structures

The WACC machine assumes a roughly constant mix. When leverage is on a planned journey — an LBO paying down debt, a big releveraging — value the pieces separately:

$$V_L=\underbrace{\sum_t\frac{FCFF_t}{(1+k_U)^t}}_{\text{value if all-equity}}+\underbrace{\sum_t\frac{t\cdot\text{Interest}_t}{(1+k_d)^t}}_{\text{tax shields, year by year}}-PV(\text{distress})$$

where the unlevered cost of equity is \(k_U=r_f+\beta_U\times ERP = 4.20\%+0.79\times5.0\%=\mathbf{8.15\%}\) for MMD. WACC-DCF and APV agree when leverage is steady; APV wins when it isn't, because each year's actual interest drives that year's shield. The model carries APV as a toggle — same forecast, alternative engine.

Where people go wrong
  • Celebrating EPS accretion as value creation.
  • Discounting tax shields at k_U while also using the permanent-debt tD shortcut — two inconsistent assumptions about shield risk. Pick one, disclose.
  • Forgetting dividends are sticky: initiating one is a commitment the market will punish you for breaking.
Check yourself
Selling shareholders win — they receive more than the shares are worth. Remaining shareholders lose: corporate cash (theirs) bought overpriced paper. EPS will still rise, which is exactly why EPS is the wrong scoreboard.
When leverage changes substantially over the forecast — e.g., an LBO deleveraging from 70% to 30% debt. A single frozen WACC misprices every year of that path; APV reprices the tax shield year by year.
Part IV · Chapter 26

Options: Payoffs, Parity, Black–Scholes & Real Options

In plain English

An option is the right — never the obligation — to transact at a pre-agreed price. A call is the right to buy at strike K; a put the right to sell at K. The asymmetry is the magic: you keep the upside and walk away from the downside, so uncertainty itself becomes valuable — the only instrument in finance where more volatility means more value. Two further surprises: an option's fair price can be derived without knowing anyone's forecast (you can replicate it with stock and borrowing — the Law of One Price again), and once you see options, they're everywhere: a factory you could expand, a project you could abandon, even the equity of a leveraged company is a call option on its assets.

Payoffs and the parity that binds them

At expiry: call pays \(\max(S_T-K,0)\); put pays \(\max(K-S_T,0)\). Before expiry, price = intrinsic value + time value. And a no-arbitrage rope ties the four basic instruments together — put–call parity:

$$C+\frac{K}{(1+r_f)^T}=P+S$$

(A call plus a safe bond paying K delivers exactly what a put plus the stock delivers: \(\max(S_T,K)\). Identical payoffs, identical price — or arbitrage.) Parity means pricing one option prices its twin for free.

Binomial pricing: the engine in one step

Let the stock move to uS or dS. A portfolio of Δ shares and borrowing B can replicate the option's two payoffs exactly — solve two equations, two unknowns — so the option must cost ΔS + B. Rearranged, the price is an expectation under risk-neutral probabilities:

$$q=\frac{(1+r_f)-d}{u-d}\qquad C_0=\frac{q\,C_u+(1-q)\,C_d}{1+r_f}$$

Note what is absent: the actual probability of up vs down, and anyone's risk appetite. Replication priced the option before opinion could. Chain many small steps and the binomial tree converges to…

Black–Scholes

$$C=S\,N(d_1)-Ke^{-rT}N(d_2)\qquad d_1=\frac{\ln(S/K)+(r+\sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma\sqrt T},\;\;d_2=d_1-\sigma\sqrt T$$

Five inputs — S, K, T, r, σ — and only one, volatility, is not observable. Hence the market's habit of quoting options in volatility: the implied volatility is the σ that makes the formula match the market price, the option market's forecast of turbulence. (The GBM of Chapter 36 is exactly the world Black–Scholes assumes.)

Two payoffs of seeing options everywhere

Equity as a call (Merton). Shareholders of a levered firm hold a call on the firm's assets with strike = the debt's face value: below it they walk away (limited liability), above it they keep the excess. Corollaries: credit spreads can be priced as the put shareholders implicitly sold to lenders (deep theory behind Chapter 22), and shareholders of distressed firms rationally love risk — volatility raises their call's value at lenders' expense (risk-shifting, from Chapter 23's agency costs).

Real options. A pilot plant that could be scaled (call on expansion), a mine that can close when prices fall (put on abandonment), the right to wait for information before committing (timing option). Static NPV undervalues flexibility-rich projects because it averages over futures the manager would never accept; option thinking restores the value of being able to choose later. A negative-NPV pilot can be a brilliantly priced call.

A one-step binomial, by hand

S = 100, K = 100, one period, u = 1.25, d = 0.80, r_f = 5%. Risk-neutral q = (1.05 − 0.80)/(1.25 − 0.80) = 0.556. Payoffs: C_u = 25, C_d = 0. Price = (0.556 × 25)/1.05 = 13.23. Check by replication: Δ = 25/(125−80) = 0.556 shares, B = −(0.556×80)/1.05 = −42.3 of borrowing → cost 55.6 − 42.3 = 13.23 ✓. Two roads, one price — no forecast required.

Part IV · Chapter 27

Raising Capital: IPOs, SEOs & the Debt Markets

In plain English

Where do the E and the D in the WACC actually come from? Companies climb a financing ladder: founders' savings → angels → venture capital (money in stages, each priced by a fresh negotiation) → the IPO, where the public is invited in → and afterwards, seasoned offerings and the vast debt markets. Each rung has its own rituals and its own famous puzzle — none more famous than IPO underpricing: new issues pop on day one with startling regularity, meaning issuers systematically leave money on the table. This chapter tours the ladder and shows where its facts plug into your model.

Equity, rung by rung

  • Venture capital: staged financing (Series A, B, C…) where each round reprices the firm. Staging is itself option logic from Chapter 26 — investors buy the right, not the obligation, to fund the next stage. Preferred terms (liquidation preferences, anti-dilution) mean the headline "valuation" overstates what common shares are worth.
  • The IPO: underwriters build a book of investor demand and set the price. The underpricing puzzle: first-day returns average ~10–20% across decades and countries. Leading explanations — compensating uninformed investors for the winner's curse (they get full allocations only of the bad deals), buying aftermarket goodwill, and underwriters favoring their clients. Either way: issuing shareholders pay for the pop.
  • SEOs (seasoned offerings): announcing a new share issue drops the price ~2–3% on average — Chapter 23's pecking order made visible. Managers issue when they privately think shares are rich; the market knows they know, and discounts accordingly (adverse selection). Rights issues sidestep some of this by offering new shares to existing holders pro-rata.

Debt, instrument by instrument

Public bonds (Chapter 18's machinery) vs bank loans (floating-rate, covenant-heavy, renegotiable) vs private placements. The fine print is where credit risk is actually managed: seniority (who gets paid first in default — senior secured → senior unsecured → subordinated → equity last), security (collateral), and covenants — promises like leverage ceilings and coverage floors that hand lenders a tripwire long before bankruptcy. Two hybrids close the loop with Chapter 26: callable bonds (the firm holds a call on its own debt — it will refinance when rates fall, so investors demand extra yield) and convertibles (the investor holds a call on the equity — so the bond carries a lower coupon).

What your model takes from all this

The share-count history (issues, buybacks, option grants) that sets diluted shares in Chapter 34; the debt schedule's maturities, rates, call features and covenants from the 10-K footnotes that drive Chapters 22 and 31; and the realistic financing assumptions for the forecast — firms at their target structure (Chapter 23) refinance maturing debt rather than paying it off, and the forward curve (Chapter 19) prices that refinancing.

The cost of the pop

A company sells 50M shares at $20 in its IPO; the stock closes day one at $25. Capital raised: 1,000. Capital that the same shares were worth by nightfall: 1,250. The 250 difference — a quarter of the proceeds — was transferred from pre-IPO owners to the lucky allocation list. Underpricing is invisible on the income statement and enormous in fact.

Where people go wrong
  • Reading a VC round's "post-money valuation" as the value of common equity — preferred terms can make it wildly so.
  • Modeling debt as repaid at maturity when the firm's history (and target leverage) says it refinances.
  • Ignoring call features when marking debt to market — a callable bond is worth less to its holder than the straight-bond math says.
Check yourself
Adverse selection: managers with inside knowledge prefer to sell new shares when they believe the price is generous. Investors, knowing this preference, treat the announcement itself as bad news about value — the pecking order's empirical fingerprint.
Callable: the issuer holds a call on the debt — investors are short an option and demand a higher yield. Convertible: the investor holds a call on the stock — they accept a lower coupon as payment for it. Same option math, opposite pockets.
Part IV · Chapter 28

Mergers, Acquisitions & LBOs

In plain English

Companies buy companies for one defensible reason: the two are worth more together than apart — synergies: duplicated costs cut, products cross-sold, tax assets used, scale achieved. But here is the brutal arithmetic of deal-making: the synergies are a fixed pie, and the premium paid to the target's shareholders is the knife. Decades of evidence say targets capture most or all of the pie; acquirers, bidding against each other with their bankers cheering, routinely overpay — the winner's curse with a press release. An LBO is the same hunt with different teeth: buy the whole company using mostly borrowed money, fix it, pay down the debt, sell.

The value equation of every deal

$$\text{Max price}=V_{\text{target, standalone}}+PV(\text{synergies})\qquad \text{Acquirer's gain}=PV(\text{synergies})-\text{Premium paid}$$

Everything else is detail. Synergies must be valued like any cash flows (Chapter 7's incremental discipline: only what changes, after integration costs, at a rate matching their risk — cost synergies are bond-like, revenue synergies are lottery-like). The premium is observable. The subtraction is merciless.

Accretion/dilution: the seductive wrong question

Bankers present whether the deal raises the acquirer's EPS. Mechanics: cash deals funded with cheap debt are usually accretive; stock deals where the acquirer's P/E exceeds the target's are accretive by arithmetic alone. But buying lower-P/E earnings mechanically lifts EPS even when the deal destroys value — the same fallacy as Chapter 25's buyback lesson, wearing a bigger suit. The only scoreboard is synergies vs premium. Cash vs stock also signals: paying with stock shares the risk with the target — and hints management thinks its own shares are rich (Chapter 27's adverse selection, again); the market reads it exactly that way.

The LBO: capital structure as the product

A sponsor buys the firm with, say, 30% equity and 70% debt. Returns come from three engines: debt paydown (every dollar of FCF that retires debt transfers straight to equity), operational improvement (margin and growth — the only engine that creates value rather than relocating it), and multiple expansion (buy at 9× sell at 11× — hope, not strategy). Because leverage is on a planned journey, the valuation engine of choice is APV (Chapter 25): unlevered value plus the year-by-year tax shields the debt schedule generates. The sponsor's math is an IRR on the equity slice; the trade-off theory (Chapter 23) explains the limits — push leverage too far and distress costs eat the shields.

One deal, two prices

MMD studies a target: standalone value 4,000; credible synergies PV 800 (500 of cost cuts net of integration, 300 of revenue at a haircut). Price A — a 600 premium (4,600 total): acquirer's shareholders gain 800 − 600 = +200. Price B — a rival bidder pushes the premium to 1,000 (5,000 total): gain = −200; MMD wins the auction and loses the war. Identical target, identical synergies; the premium decided everything. The discipline is having a walk-away number before the auction starts.

Where people go wrong
  • Banking "strategic" synergies nobody can cash-flow — if it can't be modeled, it can't be paid for.
  • Celebrating EPS accretion while NPV bleeds.
  • LBO models where returns rest on exit-multiple expansion — buying value rather than building it.
Check yourself
None — EPS rose purely because high-multiple paper bought low-multiple earnings. The combined company's value is the sum of the parts (minus fees); the acquirer's shareholders now own a fairly diluted slice of it. The accretion is an optical artifact of the P/E gap.
Enterprise value is set by operations. Retiring debt just reslices the EV pie toward equity — the equity IRR looks heroic because the slice grows, not the pie. Only operating improvement grows the pie; leverage merely concentrates whoever's share of it.
Part IV · Chapter 29

Forwards, Futures & Hedging

In plain English

A forward contract locks in today the price of a transaction that happens later — an airline fixes next year's fuel, an exporter fixes the euros she'll receive in June. The surprise of this chapter: the fair forward price has nothing to do with anyone's forecast. It comes from a recipe — buy the thing today with borrowed money and store it — whose all-in cost must equal the forward price, or arbitrage. And the deeper puzzle: Modigliani–Miller logic says shareholders can hedge for themselves, so why do firms bother? Because hedging protects the things MM assumed away: the tax bill's shape, the distance to distress, and the cash to fund next year's projects.

Pricing by carry, not by crystal ball

$$F_0=S_0\,e^{(r+u-y)T}$$

— spot, grown at the financing rate r, plus storage costs u, minus any yield the asset pays while held (dividends, or the convenience yield of holding a usable commodity). For a stock index paying dividend yield q: \(F_0=S_0e^{(r-q)T}\). The proof is a sandwich: if F were higher, sell the forward, buy spot with borrowed money, deliver at T — riskless profit; if lower, reverse it. Contango (F above spot) and backwardation (below) are therefore statements about carry, not bullishness. For currencies the same logic is covered interest parity:

$$\frac{F}{S}=\frac{1+r_{domestic}}{1+r_{foreign}}$$

Futures are forwards industrialized: exchange-traded, standardized, marked to market daily through a margin account — the credit risk of a handshake replaced by the clearinghouse.

The mechanics and the why of hedging

A hedger holding exposure to S shorts h futures contracts per unit; the variance-minimizing hedge ratio is a beta in disguise:

$$h^*=\rho\,\frac{\sigma_S}{\sigma_F}$$

— literally the regression slope of spot changes on futures changes (Chapter 12's machinery, repurposed). What survives is basis risk: jet fuel hedged with crude futures still wiggles where the two diverge. As for why firms hedge when shareholders could: (1) taxes — with a convex tax schedule, smoothing income lowers the expected bill; (2) distress — volatility raises the probability of hitting the costly zone (Chapter 23's right-hand term); (3) funding — the deepest reason: hedging guarantees the internal cash to take every positive-NPV project even in bad states, avoiding the pecking order's expensive external financing. Hedge the risks you're not paid to take, so you can keep taking the ones you are.

Two locks

Gold carry: spot $2,300/oz, r = 4.2%, storage 0.3%, one year: F = 2,300·e^{0.045} = $2,406. Any quote above: cash-and-carry arbitrage; below: reverse it. No view on gold required or implied.

MMD's euro receivable: €200M due in 6 months. S = 1.080 $/€, r_$ = 4.2%, r_€ = 2.2%. CIP: F = 1.080 × (1.021)/(1.011) ≈ 1.0907. Selling €200M forward locks $218.1M — the revenue forecast of Chapter 31 stops depending on the currency gods, and MMD's planning gains a fact where it had a distribution.

Where people go wrong
  • Reading contango as "the market expects prices to rise" — it mostly reflects interest and storage.
  • Hedging with the wrong proxy and ignoring basis risk until it bites.
  • "Hedging" with a view — once the position expresses a forecast, it is speculation wearing a safety vest.
Check yourself
The dividend yield exceeds the financing rate (q > r in F = S·e^{(r−q)T}). Holding the index pays you more than the money costs — the carry is negative, so the forward prices below spot. No bearishness required.
Shareholders can replicate the payoff but not the corporate consequences: they cannot smooth the firm's taxable income, cannot keep its rating out of the distress zone, and cannot wire it cash mid-crisis to fund a positive-NPV project. Hedging defends the firm's investment capacity — value MM's frictionless world never put at risk.
Part IV · Chapter 30

Distress, Restructuring & Corporate Governance

In plain English

What happens when the promises exceed the cash? And — the quieter question that precedes every crisis — who is actually steering the company, and for whom? This chapter covers finance's emergency room and its constitution. Distress is where Chapter 23's "expected distress costs" stop being a term in a formula and become lawyers, fleeing customers, and fire sales; governance is the set of imperfect devices — boards, incentive pay, the threat of takeover — by which dispersed owners try to keep hired managers pointed at value.

When promises exceed capacity

Two roads: a private workout (exchange offers, maturity extensions, debt-for-equity swaps — fast and cheap but plagued by holdouts who refuse concessions hoping others sacrifice first) or formal Chapter 11: the automatic stay halts creditor races, management proposes a reorganization plan, classes vote, and a cram-down can bind dissenters — the legal cure for the holdout. The waterfall of absolute priority — secured, then unsecured, then subordinated, then preferred, then equity — governs in principle; in practice equity often extracts a sliver despite being underwater, because its consent speeds the exit and delay burns everyone.

Two distress pathologies you have already met as theory now show their teeth. Debt overhang: a distressed firm finds a positive-NPV project — and rationally skips it, because the payoff flows mostly to creditors while shareholders fund it (the underinvestment cost in Chapter 23's trade-off). Risk-shifting: with equity an out-of-the-money call (Chapter 26's Merton lens), shareholders welcome volatility at lenders' expense — gambling for resurrection. Covenants exist (Chapter 27) precisely to chain these two beasts early.

Governance: the agency problem and its devices

Dispersed shareholders hire managers; managers prefer empires, perks, and quiet lives; the gap is the agency cost the whole field of governance prices. The devices, each double-edged:

  • Boards — independent directors monitor on shareholders' behalf; but CEOs influence who sits at the table that judges them.
  • Incentive compensation — equity and options align fortunes; but option-heavy pay imports Chapter 26's vega: managers gain from volatility itself, and short-vesting horizons invite earnings games.
  • The market for corporate control — a sagging stock invites the acquirers of Chapter 28; the mere threat disciplines. Defenses (poison pills, staggered boards) protect bargaining power and entrench in equal measure.
  • Activists & large owners — concentrated stakes solve the free-rider problem of monitoring; their campaigns (payouts, spin-offs, board seats) are governance by ambush.

For your valuation: governance is a multiplier on everything else. The same forecast deserves a haircut when the capital allocator has a record of empire-building at premium prices (Chapter 28's subtraction, applied to history) — and ROIC's verdict on management (Chapter 5) is the single best governance statistic in the statements.

Overhang, in numbers

A distressed firm owes 100 due next year; assets will be worth 90. A project exists: cost 10 (only shareholders would fund it), certain payoff 18 — NPV +8 for the firm. But the first 10 of payoff flows to creditors (assets 90 → 108 means creditors get their full 100 instead of 90). Shareholders pay 10 to receive 8. They decline; a value-creating project dies; creditors silently lose too. The fix in real restructurings: creditors share the project's gain (new-money carve-outs, debt-equity swaps) — rewriting the claims to revive the incentive.

Where people go wrong
  • Reading absolute priority as absolute — bargaining reality deviates, and pricing distressed claims means handicapping the negotiation.
  • Treating governance as ESG décor — it is the variable that decides whether your forecast's cash reaches you or a vanity acquisition.
  • Assuming options-heavy pay "aligns" — it aligns with volatility too.
Check yourself
To dissolve debt overhang. Forgiving 15 of face can revive investment incentives whose NPV raises the value of what creditors still hold — 85 of a living company can be worth more than 100 against a corpse. Concession is self-interest with paperwork.
Volatility-seeking (option value rises with σ — risk-shifting in a suit) and horizon games (pumping near-term results, smoothing or stretching accruals into the vesting window). Alignment requires long vesting and real downside, not just upside convexity.

Part V · Chapter 31

Forecasting the Statements

In plain English

A forecast is a story told in numbers. "Meridian's new heart-valve line wins share for three years, competitors respond, growth cools toward the industry's pace" — that sentence becomes rows of revenue, margins, and spending. The test of a good forecast isn't fortune-telling accuracy; it's internal consistency: growth must be paid for with reinvestment, margins must respect competition, and the balance sheet must balance every single year. Inconsistent forecasts are how models lie politely.

The three-horizon architecture

  • Years 1–3 — explicit: segment-level builds, known product launches, guidance-informed margins.
  • Years 4–7 — the fade: drivers glide from year-3 levels toward steady state. Competitive advantage erodes; growth fades toward GDP; ROIC fades toward WACC unless a durable moat argues otherwise.
  • Terminal — steady state: Chapter 33's territory.

Line by line

  1. Revenue: build top-down (market size × share) and bottom-up (price × volume by segment); reconcile the two.
  2. Margins: split costs fixed/variable to capture operating leverage; fade EBIT margin to a defended steady state.
  3. Working capital: forecast DSO/DIO/DPO in days (what operators actually manage), then derive ΔNWC.
  4. CapEx & D&A: maintenance capex ≈ D&A at steady state; growth capex tied to revenue adds; long-run CapEx/D&A → ~1.0–1.2.
  5. Interest expense: off the forward curve (Chapter 19) applied to the debt schedule — floating debt resets at forwards; maturing bonds refinance at forward + spread. Fixed income working inside the forecast.
  6. Balance & check: cash is the residual; run the articulation check (Chapter 3) on every forecast column.

The consistency test (run it always): implied reinvestment = (CapEx − D&A + ΔNWC)/NOPAT; pair it with the growth claimed. A forecast of 12% growth at 25% reinvestment silently claims 48% incremental ROIC — say it out loud and decide if you believe it.

Free Cash Flow & the DCF Core

In plain English

Profit is an opinion; cash is a fact — and not even all cash is "free." A business must keep feeding itself (new machines, fatter inventory) before anything is truly available to its financiers. Free cash flow to the firm (FCFF) is what's left after that feeding: the cash genuinely available to everyone who funded the company. Discount the stream of FCFF at the blended rate everyone charges (WACC), and you have priced the whole enterprise. That, in one sentence, is the DCF.

The definitions

$$FCFF_t=EBIT_t(1-t)+D\&A_t-CapEx_t-\Delta NWC_t$$

$$FCFE_t=FCFF_t-\text{Interest}_t(1-t)+\text{Net borrowing}_t$$

FCFE (cash to shareholders only, discounted at k_e) is the built-in cross-check: equity value via FCFE must reconcile to the FCFF route. When they diverge, the usual culprit is interest expense that doesn't match the debt schedule.

The discounting

$$EV=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{FCFF_t}{(1+WACC)^t}+\frac{TV_T}{(1+WACC)^T}$$

Mid-year convention: cash arrives all year, not on December 31 — using exponents (t − 0.5) lifts EV by \((1+WACC)^{0.5}\approx\) 3.9% at MMD's rate. Keep it as a disclosed toggle. Stub periods: if valuing mid-fiscal-year, discount the remaining fraction of year 1 with a fractional exponent and roll on.

Part V · Chapter 33

Terminal Value, Disciplined

In plain English

We can't forecast line items forever, so at some horizon we wave a wand and say "from here, the company settles into a steady cruise" — and we price that entire infinite cruise with the little perpetuity formula from Chapter 2. The catch: this single number usually carries 60–80% of the whole valuation, so a careless wave of the wand can manufacture any answer you like. Discipline comes from three leashes: growth can't outrun the economy, growth must be paid for with reinvestment, and extraordinary returns fade as competitors arrive.

The disciplined formula

$$TV_T=\frac{NOPAT_{T+1}\left(1-\dfrac{g}{ROIC_{term}}\right)}{WACC-g}$$

This form forces the reinvestment that growth requires via RR = g/ROIC. Never grow the last year's FCFF by g mechanically — that hides an implicit (often absurd) ROIC, exactly the inconsistency Chapter 31's check caught. The leashes: g ≤ long-run nominal GDP (≈ inflation + 1%, so 2.5–3.5%); ROIC_term fades toward WACC unless a durable moat justifies a spread — and notice that at ROIC = WACC the formula collapses to NOPAT/WACC: growth without excess returns adds nothing. A profound result worth sitting with.

Exit-multiple alternative: TV = terminal EBITDA × a peer multiple — market-anchored, but it imports today's mood into the far future. Always cross-examine: compute the growth your exit multiple implies, and the multiple your Gordon TV implies. The two methods must be able to look each other in the eye.

In plain English

Part VI · Chapter 36

Monte Carlo Simulation

In plain English

Scenarios sample three possible futures. Monte Carlo samples ten thousand. Instead of fixing "growth = 6.8%", we say "growth is a bell curve centered at 6.8%, give or take 1.5%" — and likewise for margins, the discount rate, and terminal growth. The computer rolls all the dice together (respecting that good worlds tend to be good across the board), runs the entire DCF on each roll, and hands back a full probability distribution of value. The question stops being "what's it worth?" and becomes "what are the odds it's worth more than today's price?"

The machinery

Place distributions on the tornado's top dials — e.g., revenue CAGR ~ Normal(6.8%, 1.5%); terminal margin ~ Triangular(19, 21, 22.5); β ~ Normal(0.98, 0.15) feeding k_e and WACC (so Chapter 12's estimation error is finally honored rather than ignored); terminal g ~ Normal(3.0%, 0.4%) truncated below WACC. Drivers co-move, so draws must be correlated: build the correlation matrix Σ, factor it by Cholesky (Σ = LLᵀ), draw independent normals z, transform x = Lz. Run the full Chapter 31→18 pipeline on each draw, vectorized. Accuracy improves like σ/√n: 10,000 paths pin the percentiles to within cents; fix the random seed so results reproduce.

Geometric Brownian Motion — the Hilpisch heartbeat — simulates the stock price itself:

$$S_T=S_0\exp\!\Big[(\mu-\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2)T+\sigma\sqrt{T}\,Z\Big]$$

giving a market-implied distribution to set beside your fundamental one, and opening the gate to option pricing (average the simulated payoffs under the risk-neutral drift, discount at r).

The distribution is right-skewed (growth compounds), so the mean ($36.9) sits above the median — report both. Decision framing: at $40 you need roughly the 68th percentile of fundamentals just to break even; with a 20% margin-of-safety rule, the buy trigger sits near $29.

Part VI · Chapter 37

Relative Valuation: Multiples Done Honestly

In plain English

"The house next door sold for $300 per square foot, so yours is worth about…" — that's a multiple. Fast, market-anchored, and dangerous in exactly the same way: the house next door might have a renovated kitchen (higher growth), a crumbling foundation (lower returns), or have sold in a panic (mispriced market). A multiple is a compressed DCF — every one of them secretly encodes growth, returns, and risk — so differences in multiples must be explained by differences in those three things, or they are signals.

What a multiple really is

$$\frac{EV}{NOPAT}=\frac{1-g/ROIC}{WACC-g}$$

Derived straight from the Gordon machinery: firms deserve different multiples for exactly three reasons — growth, return on capital, risk. "Cheap" on a multiple often just means low-growth or low-ROIC. The toolkit: EV/EBITDA (capital-structure-neutral workhorse; mind lease and capex-intensity differences), EV/EBIT (penalizes capex hogs properly), EV/Revenue (pre-profit firms), P/E (equity-side, leverage-contaminated), PEG (rough growth patch).

Regression-based multiples — your econometrics pointed at comps: regress EV/EBITDA on growth and ROIC across the peer set; the fitted value at your firm's fundamentals is its justified multiple; the residual is mispricing-or-omitted-variable. Materially better than "peer median."

Where people go wrong
  • Mixing trailing (LTM) and forward (NTM) numbers across the comp table.
  • Comparing lease-heavy and lease-light EBITDAs.
  • Choosing the comp set after seeing which answer it gives.
Check yourself
Unknown until you check growth, ROIC, and risk. If B grows twice as fast at higher returns, 14× may be the bargain. The formula EV/NOPAT = (1 − g/ROIC)/(WACC − g) is the adjudicator.

# Bond toolkit (semiannual)
from scipy.optimize import brentq

def price(c, T, y, F=1000):
    n, cf = int(2*T), c*F/2
    pv = sum(cf/(1+y/2)**t for t in range(1, n+1))
    return pv + F/(1+y/2)**n

def ytm(P, c, T, F=1000):
    return brentq(lambda y: price(c,T,y,F) - P, 1e-6, 1.0)

ytm(930, 0.04, 8)        # → 0.0507  (Ch. 8 ✓)

def duration(c, T, y, F=1000):
    n, cf = int(2*T), c*F/2
    flows = [(t/2, cf if t<n else cf+F) for t in range(1,n+1)]
    P = price(c,T,y,F)
    mac = sum(t*f/(1+y/2)**(2*t) for t,f in flows) / P
    return mac, mac/(1+y/2)                      # Macaulay, modified

# Bootstrapping (annual-pay par yields → spot rates, Ch. 9)
def bootstrap(par):                              # par = [0.040, 0.045, 0.048]
    spots = []
    for i, c in enumerate(par, start=1):
        pv_coupons = sum(c*100/(1+z)**(j+1) for j,z in enumerate(spots))
        z_i = ((100 + c*100)/(100 - pv_coupons))**(1/i) - 1
        spots.append(z_i)
    return spots                                 # → [4.000%, 4.511%, 4.823%] ✓
Check yourself

In plain English

# The vectorized DCF engine (simplified MMD)
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)                  # fixed seed = reproducible
n = 10_000

# correlated draws via Cholesky (Ch. 20)
corr = np.array([[1.0, 0.5, -0.2],
                 [0.5, 1.0, -0.1],
                 [-0.2,-0.1, 1.0]])              # growth, margin, wacc
L = np.linalg.cholesky(corr)
Z = rng.standard_normal((n, 3)) @ L.T

g     = 0.068 + 0.015*Z[:,0]                     # revenue CAGR
marg  = 0.21  + 0.010*Z[:,1]                     # terminal EBIT margin
wacc  = 0.0786+ 0.006*Z[:,2]
gT    = np.clip(0.03 + 0.004*rng.standard_normal(n), None, wacc-0.01)

def dcf(g, marg, wacc, gT, rev0=10_000, t=0.22, roicT=0.15):
    years = np.arange(1, 6)
    rev   = rev0 * (1+g[:,None])**years           # (n, 5) all paths at once
    nopat = rev * marg[:,None] * (1 - t)
    fcff  = nopat * 0.88                          # net reinvestment ≈ 12%
    disc  = (1+wacc[:,None])**years
    pv_ex = (fcff/disc).sum(axis=1)
    nop6  = nopat[:,-1]*(1+gT)
    tv    = nop6*(1-gT/roicT)/(wacc-gT)
    ev    = pv_ex + tv/(1+wacc)**5
    return (ev + 600 - 8_800 - 150) / 705        # the bridge → $/share

vals = dcf(g, marg, wacc, gT)
np.percentile(vals, [5,25,50,75,95]).round(1)    # ≈ [24, 31, 36, 41, 50]
(vals > 40).mean()                               # ≈ 0.32 — P(worth > price)

Forward rate — the curve's implied rate between two future dates. Hamada — the lever/unlever relation for beta. Invested capital — operating assets net of operating liabilities; the money doing the work. NOPAT — after-tax operating profit. Pecking order — finance internally, then debt, equity last. Reverse DCF — solving for the assumptions a market price implies. ROIC — NOPAT ÷ invested capital. Spot rate — yield of a pure zero-coupon bond. Synthetic rating — a credit rating implied from coverage ratios. Terminal value — the value at the horizon of all years beyond it. Trade-off theory — optimal leverage balancing tax shields against distress costs. Treasury-stock method — diluted share counting for in-the-money options. WACC — the blended after-tax required return of all capital. NPV — present value of all cash flows, in and out; the master decision rule. IRR — the discount rate at which NPV is zero. Sharpe ratio — excess return per unit of total risk. Efficient frontier — the set of portfolios with maximal return per risk. Deferred revenue — customer cash received before the work; a liability of effort owed. Put–call parity — the no-arbitrage identity C + PV(K) = P + S. Implied volatility — the σ that makes Black–Scholes match the market price. Risk-neutral probability — the pseudo-probability under which discounted prices are fair games; the binomial’s q. Underpricing — the systematic first-day IPO pop; money left on the table. Real option — managerial flexibility (expand, abandon, wait) valued as an option. APT — pricing by no-arbitrage across multiple factors. SMB / HML / RMW / CMA — the Fama–French size, value, profitability and investment factors. Information ratio — alpha per unit of tracking error. GARCH — volatility model where variance reacts to shocks and persists. VaR / Expected shortfall — the loss quantile / the average loss beyond it. Immunization — matching asset and liability durations so rate moves cancel. Swap rate — the fixed rate making a new swap worth zero. Basis risk — hedge and exposure diverging. Covered interest parity — forwards set by the interest differential. Debt overhang — underinvestment because gains accrue to creditors. Winner’s curse — auction victory through overpayment. Absolute priority — the payout waterfall in bankruptcy.
